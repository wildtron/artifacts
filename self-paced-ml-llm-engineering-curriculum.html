ML & AI Engineering Curriculum

One track in two parts · 26 courses · 232 weeks (about 4.5 years) at about 12 hours a week · Updated November 21, 2026

Program overview

This curriculum takes a learner from programming basics to the knowledge and portfolio expected of a doctoral-level ML researcher or a principal ML engineer. It is one track in two parts. Part I (Terms 1–6, 128 weeks) reaches master's level and ends in a thesis. Part II (Terms 7–10, 104 weeks) reaches doctoral and principal level and ends in a dissertation. In total: 26 courses over 232 weeks, about 4.5 years at about 12 hours a week.

Prerequisites: working knowledge of one programming language and basic Python (variables, functions, loops, classes). Learners new to Python should first work through Python Crash Course (see the reading list). High-school algebra is enough to start the math course. Learners with a computer science degree can move faster through review material.

Ordering: two job-ready milestones come early. Term 2 covers building LLM products (AI engineer level after about 9 months). Term 3 adds cloud, enterprise and customer-facing skills (AI solutions engineer level after about 14 months). Terms 4–6 build model-level depth, and Part II builds research and large-scale systems depth. Learners who prefer model-building before employability can take Term 4 before Terms 2–3; nothing in Terms 2–3 depends on Term 4.

What "doctoral or principal level" means here: Part II builds the knowledge and portfolio of someone at that level, but both titles also need outside proof. A PhD is judged by original, peer-reviewed research. A principal engineer is judged by years of technical impact across an organization. So Part II's targets are external: a paper submitted to a peer-reviewed venue, open-source work other people use, and technical leadership on real projects.

Resource policy: every resource is a book, a paper, an article or written documentation, so progress can be tracked page by page. There are no video series or online courses. Free resources are marked. The whole curriculum was last reviewed for currency in November 2026. LLM tooling, cloud product names and regulations change fast, so check each link for its current version before starting a course.

How each course works

  1. Summary — what the course is for
  2. Learning outcomes — what a learner can do after finishing
  3. Focus — where to spend extra effort, and what to skip
  4. Resources — the main text (read fully) and supporting books, papers, articles and documentation, all linked
  5. Weekly schedule — topic, reading and practice for each week
  6. Exercises — at least five, each with sample data: a small synthetic table printed in the course, or a linked public dataset
  7. Checkpoint test — every item must pass before moving on; a failed item means repeating that week's practice, not the whole course

Weekly study rhythm

Session Time Activity
Weekday × 3 1.5–2 h each Read one chapter, take notes in plain words, do the chapter exercises
Weekend block 4 h Work through the course exercises; push code to a public repository
Daily review 10–15 min Spaced-repetition flashcards made from each chapter's key ideas
Paper of the week (from Term 2) 1 h Read one paper with the three-pass method and write a one-paragraph summary; two a week from Term 7
Weekly review 30 min Explain the week's main concept aloud or in a short post (the Feynman test)

Grading: a course passes only when (1) every checkpoint item is done, (2) the exercises and projects are in a public repository with a README, (3) the core ideas can be explained without notes, and (4) a short public post explains one thing learned. A course that doesn't pass this way is repeated, not skipped. If time gets tight, compress Terms 5–6, but never skip ML 501's math.

Habits that run through every term

  • Retention: after every chapter, write 5–10 flashcards in a spaced-repetition app such as Anki and review daily. This keeps Term 1's math available for Term 7.
  • Papers: start the paper of the week in Term 2. Use the three-pass method from How to Read a Paper (S. Keshav), and keep a one-paragraph summary of each in a searchable notes file. By Term 9 this gives 150+ summaries to draw on.
  • Engineering standards: every project gets type hints, tests, a linter, continuous integration (CI) and a README with results. Coding agents are now a standard tool: use them for routine work once a topic is understood, but write each course's core exercises by hand first, and review every line an agent writes as carefully as a colleague's pull request.
  • Compute plan: free notebook GPUs (Kaggle notebooks, Google Colab) are enough through Term 4. Rent cloud GPUs by the hour for the larger projects in Terms 5 and 8. Set a monthly budget and a billing alert first.
  • Community: join a study group, online forum or local meetup, and find one mentor further along. Teaching others is the strongest test of understanding.
  • Open source: make small contributions (documentation, bug fixes) from Term 2, so the larger contribution required at Part I's graduation isn't the first.
  • Rest: take one lighter week after each term to review flashcards and catch up. Long plans fail from burnout more often than from difficulty.

ML 501 — Math for ML (Weeks 3–12)

Summary: Working intuition for the math every ML method runs on: linear algebra, calculus, probability and optimization. The goal is to read the notation and use it in code, not to write formal proofs.

Learning outcomes — by the end, a learner can:

  • Work with common distributions, expectation, variance and Bayes' theorem

Focus: the geometric picture of matrices, the chain rule and optimization. Backpropagation (DL 701) is the chain rule applied many times.

ML 502 — Statistical and classical ML (Weeks 13–24)

Summary: The standard ML workflow and the core algorithms that still dominate tabular data in industry. Most real-world ML mistakes happen in evaluation, so this course spends as much time on validation as on models.

Learning outcomes — by the end, a learner can:

  • Run an end-to-end ML project: framing, exploration, feature engineering, training, evaluation
  • Explain and apply linear and logistic regression, k-NN, decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting and SVMs
  • Use k-means clustering and PCA
  • Choose the right metric (RMSE, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC) and validate with cross-validation
  • Diagnose overfitting with the bias–variance trade-off and fix it with regularization
  • Detect and prevent data leakage

Focus: evaluation, leakage and gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM or scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingClassifier), which wins most tabular problems. Use scikit-learn Pipeline for everything.

Term 2 — Building with LLMs (Weeks 25–40)

Term goal: by week 40, ship an LLM product with retrieval, tools and an evaluation suite. This is the level of a working AI engineer. The main text for the whole term is AI Engineering by Chip Huyen, with its companion repository. Examples use Python; any backend language works for services. Exercises work with any major model provider.

AIE 601 — LLM foundations and APIs (Weeks 25–27)

Summary: A working mental model of how LLMs behave, then calling them well from code: structured output, tool calling, streaming, and managing cost and latency.

Learning outcomes — by the end, a learner can:

  • Explain tokens, context windows, temperature, embeddings and why models hallucinate
  • Call at least two providers' APIs, with streaming

DISS 1999 — Dissertation (Weeks 209–232)

Summary: An original contribution to the chosen specialization: a new method, finding, benchmark or system that others in the field can use and cite. Unlike the Part I thesis, which applies what is known, the dissertation must add something new.

What counts as a contribution:

  • A method that measurably beats strong baselines on a recognized task
  • A careful empirical study that changes how a technique is understood
  • A benchmark or dataset that fills a real gap (for example, an under-served language or domain)
  • An open-source system that others adopt, with an accompanying paper
