ML & AI Engineering Curriculum

One track in two parts · 26 courses · 232 weeks (about 4.5 years) at about 12 hours a week · Updated November 20, 2026

Program overview

This is one track in two parts. Part I (Terms 1–6, 128 weeks) takes you to master's level and ends in a thesis. Part II (Terms 7–10, 104 weeks) takes you to doctoral and principal-engineer level and ends in a dissertation. That's 26 courses over 232 weeks (about 4.5 years) at about 12 hours a week. It merges the earlier two-track roadmap with the Self-Taught MS in ML, Deep Learning & LLMs plan. You get the master's-level depth (generative models, reasoning and RL, Stanford lectures, a thesis), the practical engineering (weekly schedules, checkpoint tests, RAG, agents, evals, MLOps), and the customer-facing skills of an AI solutions engineer (cloud platforms, enterprise integration, security and compliance, discovery, demos and proposals).

The order is deliberate, with two job-ready milestones early. After the foundations, Term 2 teaches you to build LLM products, so you're employable as an AI engineer about 9 months in. Term 3 adds the cloud, enterprise and customer-facing skills of an AI solutions engineer, a second milestone at about 14 months. Terms 4–6 then take you to model-building depth, and Part II to research and large-scale systems depth.

What "PhD or principal level" means here: Part II gives you the knowledge and portfolio of someone at that level, but both titles also need outside proof. A PhD is judged by original, peer-reviewed research. A principal engineer is judged by years of technical impact across an organization. So Part II's targets are external: a paper submitted to a peer-reviewed venue, open-source work other people use, and technical leadership on real projects. Expect Part II to overlap with your job, where most principal-level growth happens.

How each course works

  1. Summary — what the course is for
  2. Learning outcomes — what you can do when you finish
  3. Focus on — where to spend extra effort, and what to skip
  4. Resources — main text (read fully), lectures to watch alongside, and supporting material, all linked
  5. Weekly schedule — topic, reading and practice for each week
  6. Checkpoint test — every item must pass before you move on

Weekly study rhythm

Session Time What you do
Weekday × 3 1.5–2 h each Read one chapter, take notes in your own words, do the exercises
Weekend block 4 h Watch the matching lecture, code the chapter, push to GitHub
Daily review 10–15 min Spaced-repetition flashcards (Anki) made from each chapter's key ideas
Paper of the week (from Term 2) 1 h Read one paper with the three-pass method and write a one-paragraph summary; two a week from Term 7
Sunday review 30 min Explain the week's concept aloud or in a short post (Feynman test)

Grading yourself: a course passes only when (1) every checkpoint test item is done, (2) its projects are on GitHub with a README, (3) you can explain the core ideas without notes, and (4) you've written a short public post about one thing you learned. A course you can't pass this way gets repeated, not skipped. If time gets tight, compress Terms 5–6, but never skip ML 501's math.

Habits that run through every term

These aren't courses. They're the practices that turn 4.5 years of study into lasting skill.

  • Retention: after every chapter, write 5–10 flashcards in Anki and review daily. Spaced repetition is the cheapest way to keep Term 1's math alive until you need it in Term 7.
  • Papers: start the paper of the week in Term 2. Use the three-pass method from How to Read a Paper (S. Keshav), and keep a one-paragraph summary of each in a searchable notes file. By Term 9 you'll have 150+ summaries to draw on.
  • Engineering standards: every project gets type hints, tests, a linter, CI and a README with results. Principal engineers are judged on code others can maintain, and habits formed in Term 1 last.
  • Compute plan: use free GPUs (Kaggle notebooks, Google Colab) through Term 4. Rent cloud GPUs by the hour for the big projects in Terms 5 and 8. Set a monthly budget and a billing alert before you start.
  • Community: join one study group or community (e.g. the GPU MODE community, Hugging Face forums, or a local AI meetup) and find one mentor ahead of you. Teach what you learn; it's the strongest test of understanding.
  • Open source: make small contributions from Term 2 (docs, bug fixes) so the larger contribution in Part I's graduation isn't your first.
  • Rest: take one lighter week after each term to review flashcards and catch up. Plans this long fail from burnout more than from difficulty.

ML 501 — Math for ML (Weeks 3–12)

Summary: Build working intuition for the math every ML method runs on: linear algebra, calculus, probability and optimization. The goal is to read the notation and use it in code, not to write proofs. This is what separates people who use models from people who understand them; your BSCS background lets you go faster where it's review.

Learning outcomes — by the end you can:

  • Treat a matrix as a transformation, and explain rank, inverses, eigenvectors, SVD and PCA
  • Take gradients and apply the chain rule through nested functions
  • Explain gradient descent and basic constrained optimization
  • Work with common distributions, expectation, variance and Bayes' theorem
  • Explain maximum likelihood estimation, confidence intervals and hypothesis tests

Focus on: the geometric picture of matrices, the chain rule and optimization. Backpropagation (DL 701) is the chain rule applied many times. Skip formal proofs.

ML 502 — Statistical and classical ML (Weeks 13–24)

Summary: Learn the standard ML workflow and the core algorithms that still dominate tabular data in industry. Most real-world ML mistakes happen in evaluation, so this course spends as much time on validation as on models.

Learning outcomes — by the end you can:

  • Run an end-to-end ML project: framing, exploration, feature engineering, training, evaluation
  • Explain and apply linear and logistic regression, k-NN, decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting and SVMs
  • Use unsupervised methods: k-means clustering and PCA
  • Choose the right metric (RMSE, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC) and validate with cross-validation
  • Diagnose overfitting with the bias–variance tradeoff, and fix it with regularization
  • Spot and prevent data leakage

Focus on: evaluation, leakage and gradient boosting (XGBoost or LightGBM), which wins most tabular problems. Use scikit-learn Pipeline for everything.

Term 2 — Building with LLMs (Weeks 25–40)

Term goal: by week 40 you have shipped an LLM product with RAG, tools and an eval suite, and you're employable as an AI engineer. The main text for the whole term is AI Engineering by Chip Huyen, with its companion repo. Build in Go where you can, and use Python only where the tooling demands it.

AIE 601 — LLM foundations and APIs (Weeks 25–27)

Summary: Get a working mental model of how LLMs behave, then learn to call them well from code: structured output, tool calling, streaming, and managing cost and latency.

Learning outcomes — by the end you can:

  • Explain tokens, context windows, temperature, embeddings and why models hallucinate
  • Call the Claude and OpenAI APIs from Go and Python, with streaming
  • Get reliable structured JSON output and use tool (function) calling
  • Use prompt caching and batching, and estimate the cost and latency of a feature

Focus on: tool calling and structured output, which every later course builds on. Use the raw SDKs rather than frameworks, so you see what's happening.

AIE 602 — Prompting and RAG (Weeks 28–32)

Summary: Learn to control model behavior with prompts and context, then ground answers in your own data with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), the most common pattern in production LLM apps.

Learning outcomes — by the end you can:

  • Write clear system prompts, use few-shot examples, and structure prompts with XML or JSON sections
  • Decide what goes into the context window and in what order (context engineering)
  • Chunk documents, generate embeddings and store them in a vector database
  • Build hybrid search (keyword plus vector) and add a reranker
  • Measure retrieval quality separately from answer quality

Focus on: retrieval quality. Most bad RAG answers come from bad retrieval, not bad prompts. Use databases you already run: pgvector in Postgres, or Valkey's vector search.

AIE 603 — Agents and MCP (Weeks 33–36)

Summary: Build systems where the model decides which tools to call and in what order. Learn the standard workflow patterns first, and when a full agent is actually needed.

AIE 604 — Evals and production (Weeks 37–40)

Summary: Learn to measure whether an LLM feature actually works, and to run it safely and cheaply in production. Systematic evals separate professional AI engineers from hobbyists.

Learning outcomes — by the end you can:

  • Build an eval set from real examples, and score outputs with code checks, human labels and LLM-as-judge
  • Run evals as regression tests in CI whenever a prompt or model changes
  • Add tracing and observability to every model call
  • Defend against prompt injection and data leakage, and add guardrails
  • Handle rate limits, retries, fallbacks between models, and caching
  • Run open models locally and compare them with hosted APIs on cost and quality

Focus on: evals. Start every feature by writing 20–50 test cases, before you write the prompt.

Term 3 — AI solutions engineering (Weeks 41–60)

Term goal: by week 60 you can run a full customer engagement on your own: discovery, solution design, security review, a working proof of concept (POC) on the customer's cloud, a demo, a proposal and a production handoff. You'll also hold one cloud AI certification.

An AI solutions engineer sits between the customer and the technology. The role goes by several titles: solutions engineer, sales engineer, solutions architect, customer engineer, forward-deployed engineer and implementation engineer. Job postings consistently ask for four things on top of Term 2's skills: cloud and enterprise infrastructure, integration with the customer's systems, security and compliance fluency, and the ability to discover needs, demo and explain trade-offs to both engineers and executives (role overview, role description). Each course below covers one of those, and SOL 655 ties them together in a real engagement.

From now on, practice out loud. Present every project from here to the end of the curriculum to someone else in under 10 minutes. It's the cheapest way to build the communication skills this role is hired for.

SOL 651 — Cloud AI platforms and infrastructure (Weeks 41–46)

Summary: Enterprise customers rarely call model APIs from a laptop. They run models inside their own cloud account, behind their own network and identity controls. Learn the three major platforms well enough to deploy the same app on any of them, and earn one certification to prove it.

Learning outcomes — by the end you can:

  • Deploy an LLM app on Amazon Bedrock, Azure AI Foundry and Google Vertex AI, and compare their model catalogs, quotas, pricing and regions
  • Use each platform's managed building blocks: knowledge bases, agents, guardrails, evaluations and batch inference
  • Apply cloud basics the security team will check: IAM roles and least privilege, VPCs and private endpoints, secrets management, encryption keys and audit logging
  • Explain data residency and data-retention options, including when a customer needs a self-hosted open model
  • Choose between serverless functions, containers and Kubernetes, and run a basic Kubernetes deployment
  • Describe infrastructure as code with Terraform, and add tracing with OpenTelemetry
  • Pass one professional-level cloud AI certification

Focus on: one cloud deeply (AWS is the most common in enterprise AI work, Azure is strongest where customers already use Microsoft 365), and the other two well enough to port an app. Spend extra time on IAM and networking; they decide most security reviews.

DISS 1999 — Dissertation (Weeks 209–232)

Summary: Make an original contribution to your specialization: a new method, a new finding, a new benchmark or a new system that others in the field can use and cite. Unlike the Part I thesis, which shows you can apply what's known, the dissertation must add something new.

What counts as a contribution:

  • A method that measurably beats strong baselines on a recognized task
  • A careful empirical study that changes how people understand a technique
  • A benchmark or dataset that fills a real gap (for example, Philippine legal-language tasks)
  • An open-source system that others adopt, with an accompanying paper
